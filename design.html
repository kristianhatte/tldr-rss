Designing for People Who are Color Blind (6 minute read)

2026-10-07T00:00:00.000Z

Color blindness affects about one in 12 men and one in 200 women, making key information, interactive elements, and color-coded data harder to perceive. Designs should pair color with an icon, pattern, or text label per WCAG 1.4.1, keep sufficient contrast, and avoid red-green and purple-blue pairings. Blue is a safer hue, though not guaranteed. Grayscale checks help, and hover, focus, and disabled states need non-color cues, ideally tested with real people.

What the 500 in Blue-500 Means (9 minute read)

2026-10-07T00:00:00.000Z

Numbers in color scales such as blue-500 may look standardized, but different design systems use them to represent entirely different concepts, including position in a ramp, guaranteed contrast relationships, measured lightness, or semantic roles. Material Design's original 50–900 scale primarily described positions and established 500 as a typical primary color, while systems such as Carbon, Material 3, and Radix give their numbers more explicit meaning. Understanding what a scale actually encodes is important because two colors carrying the same numerical label can have very different lightness, contrast, and intended uses.
